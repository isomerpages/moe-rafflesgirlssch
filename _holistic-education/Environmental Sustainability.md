<p>The RGS Green Strategic Plan maps out a holistic and whole-of-school effort
to support Singapore’s national agenda on sustainable development. It aims
to make environmental sustainability a key theme through the 4Cs: <strong>C</strong>ampus; <strong>C</strong>urriculum; <strong>C</strong>ulture
and <strong>C</strong>ommunity (e.g. partnerships beyond school.)</p>
<h3><strong>Learning Through Biophilia at RGS</strong></h3>
<p>The RGS campus lies within 1 km of the Central Catchment Nature Reserve,
one of Singapore’s most significant remnants of native rainforest. This
close connection to nature provides a valuable opportunity for the school
to support local biodiversity, national conservation plans and the global
climate crisis through deepened environmental learning.</p>
<p>The RGS <strong>Biophilic Master Plan</strong> can be put into practice
here and it demonstrates our commitment to drive environmental sustainability
in the school</p>
<p>The master plan, <em>Learning Through Biophilia – Bringing Nature into the School Campus</em>,
aims to transform RGS into a biodiverse and ecologically sustainable learning
environment. It positions the campus as a living laboratory where students
can observe ecological processes, participate in citizen science, and develop
a stronger understanding of their relationship with the natural world.</p>
<h3><strong>Rewilding at Our Wild Garden</strong></h3>
<p>Our Wild Garden is the first of several rewilded landscapes planned across
the RGS campus. Rather than creating a fixed or highly manicured garden,
the design works with natural processes and allows the landscape to change
over time.</p>
<p>The layout responds to the site’s existing topography and is anchored
by the Rain Tree. Prior to this intervention, the majority of the garden
stayed flooded after it rained each time. The topography was altered: the
edges of the garden were raised while the middle remained low, especially
around the Rain Tree. This directs rainwater to collect only in the middle
of the garden, in natural depression called a swale. Plants that tolerate
more damp conditions are planted here.</p>
<p>The garden brings together plants that represent a mosaic of habitats
inspired by Singapore’s natural landscapes, for example:</p>
<ul data-tight="true" class="tight">
<li>
<p>Grasslands with low groundcover, tall grasses and wildflowers in sunnier
areas</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Tropical scrublands featuring ferns, gingers and orchids</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>A seasonal freshwater swamp under the existing Rain Tree</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>An urban regenerated forest</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>These habitats provide different conditions for plants and wildlife. As
vegetation establishes, soils recover and the canopy develops, the garden
will continue to evolve. Seeds and wildlife may move between the campus
and the nearby nature reserve, helping the school grounds function as an
ecological stepping stone through the wider landscape.</p>
<h3><strong>A Living Landscape</strong></h3>
<p>Rewilding means giving natural processes more space to shape, renews the
landscape and and creates wildlife corridors.&nbsp; Maintenance is reduced
selectively, while targeted care is still used to support ecological health,
safety and access.</p>
<p>Change is therefore an essential part of the garden. Some plants may germinate,
flower and complete their life cycle within months, while trees and other
long-lived species gradually establish. Areas that are sunny today may
become shaded in the future, allowing different plant communities to develop.
What visitors see during one visit may not be the same on the next, and
this changing character is part of the garden’s life.</p>
<p>Paths guide visitors through the different habitat zones, while changes
in elevation, planting structure, texture and moisture create varied experiences
for people and opportunities for wildlife.</p>
<h3><strong>What Is Biophilic Design?</strong></h3>
<p>Biophilic design brings nature into human environments to support health,
learning and well-being. At RGS, this includes opportunities to experience
natural light, vegetation, water, organic forms, changing seasons and the
sounds and movement of wildlife.</p>
<p>The rewilded garden extends these principles beyond the building. It combines
the human-centred benefits of biophilic design with an ecosystem-centred
approach to rewilding—creating a campus that supports both people and urban
biodiversity. In addition, it will create an immersive nature-based learning
environment that can seamlessly integrate educational, ecological and aesthetic
considerations.</p>
<p>As the campus matures, students and visitors are invited to pause, observe
and return regularly. Look closely at how the habitats change, listen for
birds and insects, and notice how plants respond to light, rain and shade.
Through these everyday encounters, the RGS campus becomes not only a place
to learn about nature, but a place to learn <em>through</em> nature.</p>
